Look at the surface layer

  1. Each molecule on the surface is attracted to the molecules beside it, so it is pulled sideways along the surface (red arrows).
  2. Joined side to side like this, the top layer of molecules acts like a stretched sheet.
  3. A weight pushes the sheet down. The stretched sheet pulls back up and out at the edges of the weight (blue arrows), just as a trampoline mat holds up a person.
  4. Stronger attraction means a stiffer, tighter sheet, so it can carry a heavier weight before it tears.

The trampoline picture

Tick “trampoline springs”. Each sideways attraction between neighbouring surface molecules is a spring in the mat. Stiffer springs mean the mat sags less under the same person, and it takes a heavier person to tear it.

Try this:

  1. Set the force strength to 2 and add weights until the surface breaks. Note the load.
  2. Set it to 9 and repeat. Watch how the springs and the sag change.
  3. Before you start, predict the load limit at strength 5. Then check.

Weights here are idealised “mass units”. A real paper clip floats on water for the same reason, but only if placed gently.

How long does a metal ball take to sink?

Release a steel ball at the top of a column of liquid and time its fall to the bottom. Compare liquids with different force strengths.

Look inside the liquid

  1. Molecules in a liquid are close together and constantly attracted to their neighbours. They can slide past each other, but they hold on as they go.
  2. For the ball to move down, molecules in front must move aside and flow round to fill the space behind. Each molecule has to pull free from some of its neighbours.
  3. The stronger the attraction, the more each molecule resists being pulled away, so the liquid flows more slowly. That resistance to flow is viscosity.
  4. Watch the molecules near the ball: with strong forces they drag and stretch together, and the ball is held back.

Think about it

  • What would the fall time be if the molecules did not attract each other at all?
  • The ball reaches a steady speed quickly. What two forces are balanced at that point?
  • Real liquids also differ in molecular size and shape. What else could slow the ball besides attraction strength?

Energy of the molecules

Molecules in a liquid have a spread of energies. To escape into the vapour, a molecule needs enough energy to break away from its neighbours (the dashed line). Only the shaded tail can do that.

Look inside the liquid

  1. Weaker attraction means a lower escape threshold, so a bigger share of the molecules can leave. The liquid is more volatile.
  2. The molecules that leave are the fastest ones, so the energy they take with them is lost from the liquid.
  3. The molecules left behind have a lower average energy, which is a lower temperature. Evaporation is endothermic.
  4. More volatile liquid means faster evaporation and a bigger temperature drop. Molecule colour shows energy: red is high, blue is low.
