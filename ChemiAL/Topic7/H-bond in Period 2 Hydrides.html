Hydrogen bond budget

A hydrogen bond needs two things at once: an H attached to N, O or F, and a lone pair on a neighbouring molecule. Each bond uses up one of each. Count both, not just the hydrogens.

The anatomy of a hydrogen bond

Solid black line: covalent bond. Green dashed line: hydrogen bond. Orange H is the δ+ hydrogen. Blue dots are a lone pair. A hydrogen bond runs from an H to a lone pair on the next molecule, and the X–H···Y atoms sit roughly in a line. In each diagram one hydrogen bond is circled to show why it forms: the X–H bond is polar, so X is δ− and H is δ+, and the δ+ H is attracted to the δ− lone pair next door.

Counting H atoms vs counting bonds

Dashed bar: bonds if every H found a partner. Tick mark: the ceiling set by the smaller of the H count and the lone pair count. Solid bar: what the box actually produces, averaged over the last few seconds.

This is a 2D model with the same rule for every molecule. A bond counts when the H is 36–58 units from the other atom, within 40° of a straight X–H···Y line, and within 40° of the lone pair direction. Each H and each lone pair can be used once. Every molecule moves and turns within its own patch of the box, so they stay spread out evenly. Real liquids are 3D and their bonds break and re-form constantly, so the counts are time averages.

Electronegativity matters

NH3 and HF both form 1 hydrogen bond per molecule. So which bond is stronger? It depends on how positive the H is, and that depends on the electronegativity difference (ΔEN) across the X–H bond.

Rule 1: bonds per molecule

Take the smaller of the H count and the lone pair count.

H2O (2) > NH3 = HF (1)

Rule 2: if tied, compare ΔEN

A larger ΔEN leaves H more δ+, so it attracts the lone pair more strongly. HF (1.78) beats NH3 (0.84).

HF > NH3

Result

Matches the measured boiling points.

H2O > HF > NH3

Electronegativity (Pauling): H 2.20, N 3.04, O 3.44, F 3.98. Hydrogen bond energies are typical textbook values and vary by source. Boiling points at 1 atm.
