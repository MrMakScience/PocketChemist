Experiment 1 of 3

Electrons never sit still

Argon is a single atom with no permanent dipole, yet it becomes a liquid at 87 K. Something attracts the atoms to each other.

  1. Random movement. On average the electron cloud is symmetrical. At one instant the electrons are bunched to one side, so that side is δ− and the other is δ+. This is an instantaneous dipole.
  2. Induced dipole. The δ− side repels the electrons of the neighbouring atom and the δ+ side attracts them. The neighbour's cloud tilts.
  3. Attraction. δ+ now faces δ−. The dipoles change direction all the time, but they change together, so the attraction stays.

Experiment 2 of 3

More electrons, stronger pull

The halogens are all non-polar diatomic molecules, so London forces are the only attraction between them. Going down the group the number of electrons rises from 18 to 106.

  • A larger cloud has more electrons that can end up on one side, and its outer electrons are held loosely. At any moment the instantaneous dipole is bigger.
  • A bigger dipole induces a bigger dipole in the neighbour, so the attraction is stronger. More energy is needed to separate the molecules, so the boiling point rises.

Experiment 3 of 3

Same electrons, different shape

Pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane are all C₅H₁₂. Each has 42 electrons, so the size effect from experiment 2 cannot explain any difference.

Key idea. It is not how many electrons there are. It is how many are close enough to the neighbouring molecule. The attraction between two electron clouds drops off so fast with distance that electrons further away add almost nothing.

  • A long molecule lies flat against its neighbour, so many of its electrons are within range.
  • A compact, branched molecule touches at only a few points. Most of its electrons are too far away to count.
  • A larger contact surface means more electrons in range, so a stronger total attraction and a higher boiling point.

The count and the bar are for the 42 electrons of the upper molecule, which are attracted to the exposed nuclei of the lower one. The lower molecule's electrons are drawn fainter and are not counted. In a real liquid every nearby pair contributes a little, and molecules tumble, so this counts the strongest contacts as a teaching simplification.
