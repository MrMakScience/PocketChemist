The molecular view is a 3D teaching model, not a full molecular dynamics calculation. The window never changes size, the molecules never change size, and the ice lattice is never stretched. Only molecules whose centres are inside the window are counted. A molecule pushed out fades away outside the window; in real life the extra volume is taken up in every direction, which the cube shows. The ice follows the real structure of ice Ih (hexagonal ice). Its hydrogen positions are random but obey the ice rules: every molecule donates two and accepts two hydrogen bonds. The liquid hydrogen-bond network is a simplified model.

Where the open structure comes from

Each water molecule has two O–H bonds and two lone pairs on oxygen. The two hydrogens are slightly positive and each can point at a lone pair on a neighbour. The two lone pairs are slightly negative and each can accept a hydrogen from another neighbour.

That gives four hydrogen bonds per molecule, spread tetrahedrally at about 109.5°. A molecule with only four neighbours at fixed angles cannot pack tightly. The network closes into rings of six molecules with empty channels between them.

In liquid water some of these bonds are always breaking. Molecules slip into the gaps, so each one has more than four close neighbours on average. The same mass takes up less room.

Why the maximum is at 4 °C

Two effects change the volume of liquid water as it warms. Cluster collapse makes the volume fall. Random motion makes it rise. Which one is bigger depends on the temperature.

0 to 4 °C

Cluster collapse > random motion

Ice-like clusters with open gaps break down as the water warms, and molecules fall into the gaps. This shrinks the volume by more than faster random motion expands it. The volume falls and the density rises.

At 4 °C

The two effects are equal

The shrinking from cluster collapse exactly balances the expansion from random motion. The volume stops changing, so the density is at its maximum.

Above 4 °C

Random motion > cluster collapse

Few clusters are left to collapse, while the molecules keep moving faster and push each other further apart. The expansion is bigger than the shrinking. The volume rises and the density falls.

Check your understanding

Describe the structure of ice and explain why ice is less dense than liquid water. [4]

Each water molecule forms four hydrogen bonds, two using its hydrogen atoms and two using the lone pairs on oxygen. The bonds are arranged tetrahedrally, giving a giant lattice of hexagonal rings with empty space in the middle.

When ice melts some hydrogen bonds break and the lattice collapses. Molecules move into the gaps and are closer together on average, so the same mass takes up less volume and the density is higher.

4 H-bonds per molecule · tetrahedral · open lattice with gaps · bonds break on melting and molecules move closer

Calculate the volume of ice formed when 1.00 kg of water freezes. Ice has a density of 0.917 g/cm³. [2]

Volume = mass ÷ density = 1000 g ÷ 0.917 g/cm³ = 1090 cm³ (1.09 dm³).

The water before freezing occupied about 1000 cm³, so the volume rises by about 9 %. This is why sealed pipes and bottles burst when their contents freeze.

Explain why a lake freezes from the top downwards and why fish can survive the winter. [3]

Ice is less dense than water, so it floats and forms a layer at the surface. Cooling surface water that reaches 4 °C is the densest and sinks, so the bottom of the lake stays near 4 °C. The floating ice also insulates the water below, which stays liquid.

Explain why the density of liquid water is greatest at 4 °C. [3]

From 0 to 4 °C, hydrogen-bonded ice-like clusters keep breaking down and molecules fill the gaps. This reduces the volume by more than the normal thermal expansion increases it, so density rises.

Above 4 °C there are fewer open clusters left to collapse and the increasing kinetic energy of the molecules, which push each other further apart, dominates. Volume rises and density falls. At 4 °C the two effects cancel.

Predict what the density curve would look like if water formed only two hydrogen bonds per molecule. [2]

With two bonds per molecule water would form chains or rings rather than an open three-dimensional lattice. There would be no large gaps for molecules to fall into on melting, so the solid would be denser than the liquid and there would be no density maximum above the melting point.
