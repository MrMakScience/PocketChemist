Introducing molar volume

The molar volume is the volume taken up by 1 mole of a substance. For gases it has a very handy property: it is the same for every ideal gas.

Why is a gas so different from a liquid?

In an ideal gas the particles are far apart and have almost no attraction to each other, so the size and bonding of the particles hardly matter. In a liquid the particles touch and are held by bonds between molecules (for example hydrogen bonds in water). Different liquids pack differently, so each has its own molar volume.

Calculating molar volume

We can find the molar volume by experiment: react a known mass of solid, collect the gas it gives off, and measure the volume.

Collecting a gas in a gas syringe

Gas collected: 0 cm³

  1. Weigh a small mass of the solid (metal or metal carbonate).
  2. Push the plunger of the gas syringe fully in so it reads 0 cm³. Join the syringe to the flask with a delivery tube.
  3. Add the solid to the acid in the flask and quickly fit the bung.
  4. The solid dissolves and the gas pushes the plunger out.
  5. Read the gas volume when the plunger stops moving.
  6. Work out molar volume = volume of gas ÷ moles of gas.

Why use a gas syringe?

  • It measures the gas volume directly.
  • No gas dissolves, so it works for soluble gases such as carbon dioxide too.
  • It holds about 100 cm³, so use a small mass of solid.

Why is the answer not exactly 24.0?

  • Some gas escapes while the bung is being fitted.
  • The room may not be exactly 293 K and 1 atm.
  • The plunger can stick, so the reading is a little low.
  • The syringe can only be read to about ±1 cm³.
