Structural isomerism

Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different structural formulas. Their atoms are joined together in a different order.

The drawings are skeletal formulas. Every corner and every line end is a carbon atom, and hydrogen atoms bonded to carbon are not drawn. The condensed formula is written under each one.

Chain isomerism

The carbon skeleton is different: one has a straight chain and the others have branches. The functional group (here there is none, since these are alkanes) is the same.

Example: C5H12

Link to properties. More branching makes a more compact molecule with less surface contact, so the London forces are weaker and the boiling point is lower. Pentane boils at 36 °C, 2-methylbutane at 28 °C and 2,2-dimethylpropane at 10 °C.

Functional group isomerism

The atoms are arranged to form different functional groups, here a C=C double bond or a ring. The two molecules then react differently.

Example: C3H6

Link to structure. Propene has a C=C double bond, so it is an alkene. Cyclopropane has a ring of single bonds, so it is a cycloalkane.

Example: C4H8

Link to reactions. But-1-ene decolourises bromine water at room temperature. Cyclobutane does not.

How to name the type

  1. Check that the molecular formulas are the same. If they differ, the molecules are not structural isomers.
  2. Compare the carbon skeletons. A different skeleton (straight or branched) means chain isomerism.
  3. If the skeleton and the functional group are the same but the group sits at a different place, it is position isomerism.
  4. If the functional groups are different, it is functional group isomerism.

Finding every structural isomer: a step-by-step method

Guessing leads to missed and repeated answers. Sort the possibilities by parent chain first, then move the functional group along each chain.

  1. Parent chain. Start with the longest chain possible, then shorten it by one carbon at a time. Each carbon you remove becomes a branch.
  2. Branches. For each parent chain, place the branches on different carbons. A branch on an end carbon only makes the chain longer, and two positions that match when you flip the chain over are the same.
  3. Functional group. In each skeleton, put the C=C bond (or the halogen atom, or the –OH group) on every different position. Flipping the chain over gives repeats here too.
  4. Count. Add up the results from every parent chain. If rings are allowed, repeat the method for rings, starting with the biggest ring.

What is it?

Stereoisomers have the same atoms joined in the same order, so their structural formulas are identical. They differ in how the atoms sit in space. Around a C=C bond this gives two forms:

  • cis: two similar groups are on the same side of the C=C bond.
  • trans: the two similar groups are on opposite sides of the C=C bond.

Two conditions must both be true:

  • The molecule has a C=C double bond, which cannot rotate.
  • Each carbon of the C=C carries two different groups.

Example: C2H2Cl2 has three molecules

Properties differ. In the cis form the two C–Cl dipoles add up, so the molecule is polar (boiling point 60 °C). In the trans form they cancel, and the boiling point is lower (48 °C).

The left carbon of 1,1-dichloroethene has two identical Cl atoms, so swapping its groups gives the same molecule. It fails the second condition and has no cis-trans form. It is a structural isomer of the other two.

Why a double bond cannot twist

A C=C bond has two parts. The σ bond is end-on overlap along the line between the carbons. The π bond is sideways overlap of two p orbitals, one lobe above and one below the plane of the molecule. To twist one end, the p orbitals have to turn away from each other, and the π bond breaks.

Why C2H4Cl2 (1,2-dichloroethane) has no cis-trans form

The two carbons are joined by a single σ bond only. End-on overlap looks the same from every angle, so one CH2Cl group spins freely against the other without any bond breaking. A drawing with the Cl atoms on the same side and a drawing with them on opposite sides are two snapshots of one molecule. There is only one 1,2-dichloroethane. Its structural isomer, 1,1-dichloroethane, is a different substance.
