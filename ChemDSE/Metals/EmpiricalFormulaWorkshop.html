Empirical Formula Workshop

Four question types, each with a worked demo and practice questions. Every answer uses the same table. Relative atomic masses are always given, so keep the periodic table closed.

Why is it called “empirical”?

Empirical means found by experiment. Nobody can count atoms in a sample, so we weigh the elements instead. The masses give us moles, and the moles give the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. That ratio is the empirical formula.

The table method

Every question in this workshop is solved with the same table. Only the first step changes.

  1. 1 · MassGet the mass (g) of each element.
  2. 2 · Molesmass ÷ RAM
  3. 3 · RatioDivide the larger moles by the smallest moles.
  4. 4 · Whole numbersMultiply if any value ends in .5, .33, .67, .25 or .75.
If the question gives % → pretend the sample is 100 g → % becomes g.

Questions

Heat it, weigh it, subtract

The question gives you two masses. One piece is missing, and you find it by subtracting. Then use the same table.

Metal burns to form an oxide mass of O = mass of oxide − mass of metal

Table columns: metal and O. Divide by RAM.

Hydrated crystals are heated mass of H₂O = mass of crystals − mass of dry solid

Table columns: dry salt and H₂O. Divide by Mr. The water value in the ratio row is n.

See it with particles

Each picture shows the same sample on a balance before and after heating. The only thing that changes is the missing piece you find by subtraction.

Watch out

Mr means “add up the RAMs” for the whole formula. For H₂O: (2 × 1.0) + 16.0 = 18.0. The Mr of the dry salt is worked out for you step by step.

“Heat to constant mass” means heating, cooling and weighing again until the mass stops changing, so the reaction is complete.

Questions

Burn it and catch the products

The mass gained by each U-tube equals the mass of that product. Some questions give you the weighings instead of the product masses, so subtract first:

mass of H₂O = U-tube ① after − U-tube ① before mass of CO₂ = U-tube ② after − U-tube ② before

Then use the products to find the elements:

mass of C = 12.0 ÷ 44.0 × mass of CO₂ mass of H = 2.0 ÷ 18.0 × mass of H₂O mass of O = mass of sample − mass of C − mass of H

Check for oxygen

If C + H already adds up to the sample mass, there is no oxygen. The compound is a hydrocarbon and your table only needs C and H. Never take oxygen from the CO₂ or H₂O, because some of that oxygen came from the air.
