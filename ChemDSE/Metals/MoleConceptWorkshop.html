Learn Why we count in moles

Why do we need the mole?

One carbon-12 atom has a mass of only 1.99 × 10−23 g. No balance can weigh that, and nobody can count atoms one by one. So chemists count atoms in one giant bundle and weigh the bundle. Drag the slider to add carbon-12 atoms.

Key link: 1 mole of carbon atoms weighs 12.0 g. 1 mole of oxygen atoms weighs 16.0 g. The mass of one mole in grams is the same number as the RAM. You will use this in Tab 2.

The Avogadro constant, L

L = 6.02 × 1023 mol−1 is the number of particles in 1 mole. It is the number of atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12.

The particles can be atoms, molecules, ions, formula units or electrons. Always say which one you are counting.

n = N ÷ L   |   N = n × L

Calculator checklist for the weak spots

1. Write the formula first. n = m ÷ M, or m = n × M. Then put numbers in with units.
2. Use the brackets. For a formula like Mg(NO3)2 the 2 outside multiplies everything inside.
3. Powers of 10. Type 3.01 EXP 24 (or ×10x), not 3.01 × 10 ^ 24 by hand. Put the bottom line in brackets.
4. Round only at the end. Keep the full calculator value in the middle, then give 3 significant figures.
5. Check backwards. If n = m ÷ M, then M × n should give m again.
6. Same number of molecules? That means the same number of moles. Find moles first.

Percentage by mass

% of an element = (atoms of the element × RAM) ÷ molar mass of the compound × 100%

Pure compound

Use the formula above. Add up all the RAMs for M, then take the part you want.

Impure sample

Pretend the sample is 100 g. A 60% share means 60 g. Find the grams of the element, then the percentage is that number.

Unknown RAM

Call the unknown RAM x. Write M using x, put it in the percentage equation, and solve step by step.
