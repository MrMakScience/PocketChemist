Reacting Mass Lab

Use an equation as a recipe. Move between moles and mass, find which reactant runs out first, then compare what you should get with what you actually get. Every question lists the RAM values you need and has a step-by-step help button.

Intro to reacting mass

Think of a sandwich

2 bread + 1 cheese + 1 ham → 1 cheese-ham sandwich

The recipe is a ratio. To make 2 sandwiches you need double of everything, so 4 bread, 2 cheese and 2 ham. You never need to weigh anything: you just scale the recipe. Pick what you know and the rest follows.

A chemical equation is a recipe in moles

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O

Read the coefficients as moles: 2 mol of H2 plus 1 mol of O2 makes 2 mol of H2O. If 0.5 mol of O2 reacts, the recipe is scaled by 0.5, so you need 1.0 mol of H2 and you make 1.0 mol of H2O. Try other amounts below.

moles wanted = moles given ÷ coefficient given × coefficient wanted

Trick: divide the whole equation by the coefficient of the species you know, so that species becomes 1. Every other coefficient then reads “this much per 1”. Finally multiply the whole equation by the moles you really have, and every coefficient turns into moles.

Rewrite the equation

4NH3 + 5O2 → 4NO + 6H2O

You know how much of one species you have. Press Next step to divide the whole equation so that species becomes 1, then multiply it back up to the moles you have. Change the given species to see it work for any of them.

moles = mass ÷ MrUse when you are given a mass in grams.
mass = moles × MrUse at the end when the question asks for grams.
Mr = add up the RAMsCount every atom. For Al2O3: 2 × 27.0 + 3 × 16.0 = 102.0.

Limit and excess

Which ingredient runs out first?

2 bread + 1 cheese + 1 ham → 1 sandwich

You have 10 bread, 3 cheese and 8 ham. Bread and ham look plentiful, but the cheese is gone after 3 sandwiches. The cheese is the limiting ingredient. Bread and ham are in excess. Drag the sliders: adding more bread or ham changes nothing about how many sandwiches you can make, but adding one more cheese does.

The same idea with moles

2H2 + O2 → 2H2O

With 6 mol of H2 and 2 mol of O2, the H2 looks like plenty, but O2 is used up first. The amount of the limiting reactant decides how much product forms.

Quick check: moles ÷ own coefficient. The smallest answer is limiting.

Why it works: dividing by the coefficient tells you how many full batches of the recipe each reactant can supply. To turn batches into moles of product you would multiply by the product’s coefficient, but that multiplies every reactant by the same number, so you can skip it when you only want to compare.

Method

  1. Change each mass into moles (mass ÷ Mr).
  2. Divide each number of moles by its own coefficient and compare.
  3. The smallest result is the limiting reactant. The others are in excess.
  4. Use only the limiting reactant when you work out the product, or how much excess reactant is used.

Percentage yield

Theoretical versus actual

2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s)

Burn 4.8 g of magnesium in plenty of oxygen. The equation predicts the most MgO you could ever get: 8.0 g. That is the theoretical yield. It is a calculation, nothing is weighed. In the lab you collect the MgO and weigh it on a balance. That is the actual yield. Drag the slider to change what the balance shows and watch the percentage yield change.

percentage yield = actual yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100

Theoretical yield is the mass of product the equation predicts from the limiting reactant. Actual yield is the mass you really collect in the lab.

Roadmap for every question

  1. ÷ Mr for each reactantMoles of each reactant
  2. ÷ own coefficient, smallest winsLimiting reactant
  3. ratio from the limiting reactantMoles of product
  4. × Mr of productTheoretical yield

Then either work out percentage yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100, or work out actual yield = percentage × theoretical. With two products, find the percentage yield from the product you are given, then use it on the other one.

Why is the yield not 100%?

Below 100%

  • The reaction is reversible and does not go to completion.
  • Side reactions make other products instead.
  • Some product is lost when transferring, filtering, washing or evaporating.
  • Some reactant or product sticks to the apparatus.
  • The reactants were impure, so there was less of the real reactant than the mass suggests.

Above 100%

  • The product is not fully dry. Water or solvent adds to the mass.
  • The product is impure. It still contains leftover reactant or a by-product.
  • Weighing errors, such as forgetting to zero the balance or weighing the container.
  • A calculation slip, such as using the excess reactant instead of the limiting one.
